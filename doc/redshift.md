## TL;DR

Two physically distinct mechanisms can redshift light from a distant
galaxy: the galaxy's own motion through space (Doppler) and the
expansion of space itself (cosmological). For a single galaxy, an
observation cannot tell them apart. With many galaxies *and* an
independent distance estimate per galaxy, a hierarchical Bayesian fit
recovers both effects jointly. We demonstrate this on synthetic data,
ablate the model down to its leanest viable form, find one tempting
simplification that quietly biases $H_0$ by 4%, and end with the case
where the distance proxy is removed — so the underlying degeneracy
becomes visible.

## 1 The question

A spectral line emitted at wavelength $\lambda_0$ and observed at
wavelength $\lambda$ has redshift $z = (\lambda - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0$.
Two physical mechanisms can make $z > 0$:

- **Doppler.** The galaxy moves *through* space at some line-of-sight
  velocity $v$. For non-relativistic motion, the redshift contribution
  is $z_\text{pec} \approx v/c$.
- **Cosmological.** The metric of space itself expanded between
  emission and observation, stretching the wavelength of the photon
  while it was in flight. At low redshift,
  $z_\text{cos} \approx H_0 \, d / c$, where $d$ is the proper distance
  to the galaxy and $H_0$ is the Hubble constant.

Both effects multiply every wavelength by the same factor: line
*ratios*, the *pattern* of absorption and emission features, and the
overall *shape* of a spectrum are preserved — only the absolute
wavelength scale shifts. A spectrum recorded today and the same
spectrum redshifted by either mechanism are translations of each
other in $\log\lambda$. There is no purely spectroscopic feature that
distinguishes "the galaxy moved" from "the universe expanded".

Composition is also multiplicative: each effect scales wavelength by
a factor, one applied at the source and the other accumulated during
propagation, so the total stretch is the product of the two,

$$
1 + z_\text{obs} \;=\; (1 + z_\text{cos})\,(1 + z_\text{pec}).
$$

For one galaxy this is a single equation in two unknowns ($d$ and
$v$). It is impossible to tell "far galaxy in a peaceful universe"
from "near galaxy rushing away".

The way out, intuitively, is asymmetry between the two effects. The
cosmological piece is a *deterministic function of distance*: if you
know $d$, you know $z_\text{cos}$ exactly (given $H_0$). The Doppler
piece is *random noise* drawn from a population velocity
distribution. Plot many galaxies' redshifts against an independent
distance estimate; the slope of the cloud is the Hubble flow, and the
scatter of the cloud around the slope is peculiar motion. Slope
constrains $H_0$, scatter constrains $\sigma_v$.

This document makes that intuition precise on a small toy.

## 2 The generative model

We pretend the universe is recent enough and shallow enough that
linearized cosmology is fine: $z \le 0.1$ everywhere. A galaxy's data
$(z_{\text{obs},i}, \mu_{\text{obs},i})$ is generated by:

1. Draw a proper distance $d_i$ from a uniform-in-volume distribution
   $p(d) \propto d^2$ on $[d_\text{min}, d_\text{max}]$.
2. Draw a peculiar velocity $v_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_v^2)$
   along the line of sight.
3. Set $z_{\text{cos},i} = H_0 \, d_i / c$ and
   $z_{\text{pec},i} = v_i / c$.
4. Combine multiplicatively:
   $z_{\text{obs},i} = (1 + z_{\text{cos},i})(1 + z_{\text{pec},i}) - 1$.
5. Compute the luminosity distance with a first-order correction,
   $$d_{L,i} = d_i \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - q_0)\, z_{\text{cos},i}\right),$$
   where $q_0 \approx -0.55$ is the deceleration parameter at $z = 0$
   in our universe. This term accounts for the fact that what an
   observer measures with a standard candle is luminosity distance,
   not proper distance, and the two differ at second order in $z$.
6. Compute the distance modulus
   $\mu_{\text{true},i} = 5 \log_{10} d_{L,i} + 25$ (with $d_L$ in Mpc).
7. Add Gaussian noise:
   $\mu_{\text{obs},i} = \mu_{\text{true},i} + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\text{obs}^2)$.

The distance modulus $\mu = m - M$ is the difference between a
standard candle's apparent magnitude $m$ (how bright it looks) and
its absolute magnitude $M$ (how bright it would look at a fixed
reference distance of 10 pc). If $M$ is calibrated externally — for
example, by an outside argument that says "all Type Ia supernovae
peak at $M \approx -19.3$" — then observed brightness gives a
distance. The catch: real candles have intrinsic scatter ($\sigma_M$,
the candles are not all *quite* the same) and the apparent
brightness has measurement noise ($\sigma_\text{meas}$). These are
degenerate in the inference; we collapse them into one
$\sigma_\text{obs} = \sqrt{\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_\text{meas}^2}$.

**Defaults.** $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, $\sigma_v = 300$ km/s,
$\sigma_M = 0.10$ mag, $\sigma_\text{meas} = 0.05$ mag (so
$\sigma_\text{obs} \approx 0.11$), $d \in [20, 400]$ Mpc, $N = 500$
galaxies, $q_0 = -0.55$. These match a modern Type Ia supernova
survey at low redshift in rough order of magnitude.

**Counterfactual knobs.** Setting $\sigma_v = 0$ removes peculiar
motion entirely; observed redshifts are pure Hubble flow plus tiny
$\sigma_\text{obs}$. Setting $H_0 = 0$ removes expansion; redshifts
are pure Doppler. Removing the $\mu$ observations entirely brings
back the original one-galaxy degeneracy at the population level — see
Section 7.

## 3 The inference task

Given $\{(z_{\text{obs},i}, \mu_{\text{obs},i})\}_{i=1}^N$, we want
the posterior over $(H_0, \sigma_v, \sigma_\text{obs})$. The proper
distance $d_i$ of each galaxy is a *latent* parameter — observed
indirectly through both data channels. We treat it as a variable to
sample over rather than a quantity to plug in; this propagates
distance uncertainty through to the population parameters honestly.
(Appendix B contrasts this with three other plausible choices.)

The full joint density is

$$
p(H_0, \sigma_v, \sigma_\text{obs}, \{d_i\} \mid \text{data})
\;\propto\;
\pi_0(H_0)\,\pi_0(\sigma_v)\,\pi_0(\sigma_\text{obs})\,
\prod_{i=1}^N p(d_i)\,
p(z_{\text{obs},i} \mid d_i, H_0, \sigma_v)\,
p(\mu_{\text{obs},i} \mid d_i, \sigma_\text{obs}),
$$

with $p(d_i) \propto d_i^2$ on $[d_\text{min}, d_\text{max}]$ and
weakly informative priors $\pi_0$. The redshift likelihood deserves
a moment. Given $d_i$ and $H_0$, the implied peculiar velocity is

$$
v_i \;=\; c\!\left(\frac{1 + z_{\text{obs},i}}{1 + z_{\text{cos},i}} - 1\right),
$$

with prior $v_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_v^2)$. The change of
variables from $v$ to $z_\text{obs}$ at fixed $d$ contributes a
Jacobian $|\mathrm{d}z_\text{obs}/\mathrm{d}v| = (1 + z_\text{cos})/c$,
which in the model adds $-\log(1 + z_\text{cos})$ per galaxy to the
log-target.

Sampling is done with Stan via NUTS, 8 chains, 1000 warmup + 1000
sampling draws each, `adapt_delta = 0.95`. A short refresher on
diagnostics is in Appendix A.

## 4 Recovery on the complete model

![Joint posterior on $(H_0, \sigma_v, \sigma_\text{obs})$. Dashed red marks the truth on each marginal; red ×s mark truth in the 2-D panels.](figures/complete/posterior.png){width=70%}

Posterior summaries (median; 5th and 95th percentiles in brackets;
truth in parentheses):

| parameter | recovered | truth |
|---|---|---|
| $H_0$ [km/s/Mpc] | $70.04\;[69.80,\,70.27]$ | $70.0$ |
| $\sigma_v$ [km/s] | $324\;[231,\,450]$ | $300$ |
| $\sigma_\text{obs}$ [mag] | $0.113\;[0.105,\,0.122]$ | $0.112$ |

The Hubble diagram is what the eye expects: galaxies on a curve, with
visible scatter that has two sources (peculiar velocity and candle
noise), both apparent in the residuals.

A subtle but real feature is visible in the corner plot: $\sigma_v$
and $\sigma_\text{obs}$ are mildly anti-correlated. They share a
budget of "scatter not explained by the Hubble line". $\sigma_v$
spreads $z_\text{obs}$, while $\sigma_\text{obs}$ spreads
$\mu_\text{obs}$, but both translate through the model into
unexplained variance around the fit. The data apportions the variance
jointly; it cannot perfectly separate the two sources.

Diagnostics: no divergent transitions, $\hat{R} \le 1.07$ on the
worst parameter ($\sigma_v$), bulk effective sample size $\ge 100$ on
all of $(H_0, \sigma_v, \sigma_\text{obs})$, but a low E-BFMI
($\approx 0.07$) — the canonical signature of a centered
hierarchical funnel between $\sigma_v$ and the latent $\{d_i\}$.
Discussed further in Appendix A. Posterior coverage of truth is
correct.

### How many galaxies?

$N = 500$ is the working size everywhere else in this document; it
matches the rough order of magnitude of present-day low-redshift
Type Ia supernova samples (Carnegie Supernova Project ~300 in
total, Pantheon+ a few hundred at $z < 0.1$). To check how much that
choice matters, we re-fit the complete model at $N \in \{50, 200,
500, 2000\}$ on catalogs drawn from the same generative settings:

| $N$ | $H_0$ 90% CI | $\sigma_v$ 90% CI | $\sigma_\text{obs}$ 90% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50  | [69.5, 70.8] | [86, 737]  | [0.063, 0.124] |
| 200 | [69.4, 70.2] | [250, 600] | [0.094, 0.125] |
| 500 | [69.8, 70.3] | [231, 450] | [0.105, 0.122] |
| 2000| [69.9, 70.1] | [242, 330] | [0.109, 0.116] |

CI widths shrink as $1/\sqrt{N}$, as expected for a population
parameter. At $N = 50$ the inference on $\sigma_v$ is essentially
"there are peculiar velocities, somewhere between roughly 100 and 700
km/s" — hardly a measurement. At $N = 200$ it's a real but loose
constraint with a high-side bias. At $N = 500$, $\sigma_v$ is
adequately pinned and centered on truth; this is the working sweet
spot for a pedagogical demonstration. At $N = 2000$, $\sigma_v$ is
*precise* (±27 km/s, ~10%), and approaching what next-generation
surveys (Rubin / LSST, Roman) will deliver. The substantive
identifiability claim — "many galaxies plus a distance proxy
disentangle the two redshifts" — is robust across all four $N$, but
how informative the recovered $\sigma_v$ is depends sharply on how
many galaxies you actually have.

## 5 Ablation chain

Methodology: build the model rich, then simplify one knob at a time.
Keep a simplification only if the posterior on $(H_0, \sigma_v)$ still
covers the truth and the credible-interval widths don't change
qualitatively. A failed safety check pinpoints which feature of the
physical model the data actually leans on.

We try three simplifications, in order:

1. **Multiplicative → additive redshift.** Replace
   $1 + z_\text{obs} = (1 + z_\text{cos})(1 + z_\text{pec})$ with the
   linear $z_\text{obs} \approx z_\text{cos} + z_\text{pec}$. The
   dropped cross term $z_\text{cos} z_\text{pec}$ is at most
   $\sim 10^{-4}$ at our redshifts. The Jacobian becomes a constant
   and falls out. Posterior:
   $H_0 = 70.04\,[69.81,\,70.27]$,
   $\sigma_v = 339\,[247,\,467]$. **Safe.**

2. **Drop the $q_0$ correction** ($d_L = d$). Looks small at $z=0.1$
   — the correction is only $\sim 8\%$. Defer to Section 6.

3. **Treat $\sigma_\text{obs}$ as known.** Justified when intrinsic
   candle scatter and measurement noise have been calibrated
   externally. Posterior on $H_0$ and $\sigma_v$:
   $H_0 = 70.04\,[69.79,\,70.30]$,
   $\sigma_v = 354\,[284,\,447]$. **Safe**, with $\sigma_v$ shifting
   slightly upward as expected — variance that was being absorbed by
   $\sigma_\text{obs}$ now goes into $\sigma_v$.

![Marginal posteriors on $H_0$ (left) and $\sigma_v$ (right) across the safe ablation chain. The three safe configurations are essentially indistinguishable on $H_0$. The orange "no $\mu$" curve is Section 7.](figures/compare/marginals_safe.png){width=100%}

The three safe configurations sit on top of each other on $H_0$,
peaked at the truth. On $\sigma_v$, fixing $\sigma_\text{obs}$ shifts
the marginal a little to higher values, as just discussed. The orange
no-$\mu$ curve is the punchline of Section 7.

**The minimum we land on**, then, is: redshift combined *additively*,
the $q_0$ correction *retained* (Section 6 explains why), and
$\sigma_\text{obs}$ treated as a *known constant* calibrated
externally. Two free population parameters ($H_0$ and $\sigma_v$),
one known nuisance ($\sigma_\text{obs}$), and $N$ latent per-galaxy
distances. This is the working configuration for everything that
follows; real cosmology fits add Malmquist correction, candle
calibration uncertainty, and peculiar-velocity correlations on top of
this skeleton, but the structure is the same.

## 6 Counter-example: the $q_0$ pitfall

The $q_0$ correction multiplies proper distance by $\sim 1.08$ at
$z = 0.1$. The shift in distance modulus is
$5 \log_{10}(1.08) \approx 0.17$ mag. This is, by raw size, smaller
than $\sigma_\text{obs} \approx 0.11$ on a single galaxy — *one*
candle's typical scatter. It feels like a comfortably safe
simplification.

It is not. The model has a free parameter, $H_0$, that scales
distance through $z_\text{cos} = H_0 d / c$. When the model uses $d$
instead of $d \cdot 1.08$ for luminosity, the fit compensates by
shrinking $H_0$ until the predicted $5 \log_{10}(c\,z/H_0)$ tracks
the data. The residual that the $q_0$ curvature was explaining moves
into $\sigma_v$.

![The drop-$q_0$ ablation (vermillion) does not fit the data correctly even though the diagnostics look fine. The posteriors on $H_0$ from the additive model (bluish green, peaked on truth at 70.0) and the drop-$q_0$ model do not even overlap. $\sigma_v$ doubles to absorb the residual variance.](figures/compare/marginals_counter.png){width=100%}

In numbers: $H_0 = 67.76\,[67.46,\,68.06]$,
$\sigma_v = 668\,[588,\,750]$,
$\sigma_\text{obs} = 0.123\,[0.115,\,0.132]$. Compared to the
safe-chain $H_0 \approx 70.04 \pm 0.14$, the drop-$q_0$ posterior is
$\sim 13$ standard deviations away from truth.

The lesson is general. A "small" approximation in a function with a
free parameter is not the same thing as a small effect on inference:
the parameter can absorb the approximation silently. The
drop-$q_0$ fit's diagnostics are *clean* — no divergences, $\hat{R}
\approx 1.00$, ESS in the thousands. Stan happily reports a
well-mixed posterior centered on a wrong answer. Without an
out-of-band sanity check (in this toy: the truth, since we generated
the data ourselves), the biased number could have been published.

This is also the moral case for *complete-then-ablate* methodology
over *minimal model first*: had we started with $d_L = d$, we would
never have noticed.

## 7 The punchline: no $\mu$

Drop the distance-modulus likelihood entirely. Now the only data is
$\{z_{\text{obs},i}\}$, and the model is

$$
p(H_0, \sigma_v, \{d_i\} \mid \{z_\text{obs}\})
\;\propto\;
\pi_0(H_0)\,\pi_0(\sigma_v)\prod_i p(d_i)\,
p(z_{\text{obs},i} \mid d_i, H_0, \sigma_v).
$$

There is no longer any direct constraint on the distance to any
particular galaxy.

Naively, one expects this to be hopelessly degenerate: the original
"one equation, two unknowns" ambiguity from Section 1 is back, in
principle. The result is more nuanced.

![Joint posteriors on $(H_0, \sigma_v)$. The three safe ablations (blue, bluish green, reddish purple) cluster tightly on truth. Without $\mu$ data (orange), the cloud is markedly tilted along an anti-correlated ridge — high $H_0$ trades with low $\sigma_v$ — but is not collapsed onto a flat line.](figures/compare/joint_safe_vs_no_mu.png){width=85%}

Posterior: $H_0 = 70.21\,[69.75,\,70.61]$,
$\sigma_v = 292\,[178,\,481]$. Both parameters still cover truth.
The credible interval on $H_0$ is roughly twice as wide as in the
data-rich case; on $\sigma_v$, three times as wide. The joint
posterior is visibly tilted — high $H_0$ trades against low
$\sigma_v$ — exactly the degeneracy direction the model structure
predicts.

Why does inference still work, even partially? Because the volume
prior on $d$ — $p(d) \propto d^2$ on $[d_\text{min}, d_\text{max}]$
— quietly imports a great deal of information. The largest
$z_\text{obs}$ in the catalog roughly fixes $H_0\, d_\text{max}/c$,
the smallest fixes $H_0\, d_\text{min}/c$, and the shape of the
distribution between those endpoints constrains $\sigma_v$.

Take away the bounded distance prior — make $d_\text{max}$ unknown,
or use a prior that doesn't peak — and the joint posterior becomes
a nearly flat ridge, recovering the degeneracy in its purest form.

The honest moral: identifiability of $(H_0, \sigma_v)$ from
redshifts alone depends sensitively on what one is willing to assume
about the spatial distribution of galaxies. A distance proxy turns
the inference from "depends on assumptions" into "robust to
assumptions". That is the point of standard candles, and that is
the disentangling claim, made precise.

## 8 Caveats

The toy is intentionally small. Things real cosmology has to deal
with that we ignored:

- **Selection / Malmquist bias.** Brighter objects are
  over-represented at large distances. Doesn't break the
  identifiability claim, but biases population estimates if not
  modeled.
- **Peculiar velocity correlations.** Galaxies near a cluster fall
  toward it together; peculiar velocities are not iid Gaussian.
  Strengthens the "you can't separate this from real Hubble flow" case
  at small scales, especially within a few Mpc of a massive cluster.
- **Higher-redshift cosmology.** At $z \gtrsim 0.3$ the linearization
  fails and one needs the full Friedmann–Lemaître machinery.
- **Gravitational redshift, the CMB dipole, K-corrections,
  systematic survey effects.** Real and important; none of them
  affect the toy's central claim.
- **Real standard candle calibration.** We treated absolute
  magnitudes as calibrated. The "distance ladder" that real cosmology
  uses to anchor candles is itself a hierarchical inference problem.

The takeaway claim — that a distance proxy plus many galaxies
disentangles the two redshift mechanisms — is robust to all of the
above, provided the proxy is honest. The toy demonstrates the
*mechanism* of the disentangling, not the difficulty of the real
problem.

## Appendix A — MCMC diagnostics, briefly

A quick refresher on the four diagnostics referenced in the body.

- **$\hat{R}$ (R-hat).** Compares within-chain to between-chain
  variance of each parameter across multiple independently-initialized
  chains. Values $\le 1.01$ mean the chains have mixed and converged
  to the same distribution. Values above $1.05$ are a red flag. We
  see $\hat{R} \le 1.07$ on $\sigma_v$ in the complete model, on the
  marginal-but-acceptable line, and clean $\hat{R}$ everywhere else.
- **ESS (effective sample size).** The number of effectively
  independent samples in the posterior, after correcting for
  autocorrelation. ESS $\ge 100$ per chain is a common rule of
  thumb. Reported in two flavors: *bulk* (for posterior means) and
  *tail* (for extreme quantiles).
- **Divergent transitions.** When NUTS's leapfrog integrator
  accumulates an energy error past a threshold, the step is flagged
  as divergent. Divergences cluster in regions of the posterior
  whose curvature is too sharp for the current step size; biased
  sampling typically follows. Zero is the goal.
- **E-BFMI.** Energy-Bayesian-fraction-of-missing-information
  measures how well the sampler's momentum resampling explores the
  energy distribution. Values below $\sim 0.3$ indicate poor
  energy-axis mixing, classically a sign of a hierarchical funnel.

The complete-model fit shows $\text{E-BFMI} \approx 0.07$ — a real
funnel between $\sigma_v$ and the latent $\{d_i\}$. We tried a
non-centered reparameterization (parameter is the standardized
peculiar velocity $v_\text{raw}$, distance derived) which cleanly
escapes the funnel but introduces a hard boundary: the implied
$z_\text{cos}$ can go negative when $v_\text{raw}$ drifts large,
blowing up $\log d_L$. Bounding $v_\text{raw}$ per-galaxy by the data
is the natural fix but adds machinery the toy doesn't need. The
centered version recovers truth correctly; we accept the funnel
warning. The lower-$z_\text{obs}$ ablations (additive,
$\sigma_\text{obs}$ fixed) have noticeably better diagnostics for the
same reason — slightly less non-linear geometry.

The diagnose output for each fit is in
`build/summaries/{tag}.diagnose.txt`.

## Appendix B — Estimation methods we didn't use

Four practical paths from $\{(z_{\text{obs},i},
\mu_{\text{obs},i})\}$ to estimates of $(H_0, \sigma_v)$:

1. **Plug-in regression.** Compute $\hat{d}_i = 10^{(\mu_i - 25)/5}$
   per galaxy, then regress $z_\text{obs}$ on $\hat{d}$ with ordinary
   least squares. The classical Hubble diagram. *Biased*: $\hat{d}$
   has noise, leading to regression dilution; the log transform makes
   the noise asymmetric; uncertainty in $\hat{d}$ doesn't propagate
   to the slope. Cheap and historically dominant.

2. **Errors-in-variables / Deming-style regression.** Frequentist
   correction for noise in both axes. Recovers $H_0$ unbiasedly under
   linear-Gaussian assumptions. Doesn't give a posterior. This was
   the serious classical-cosmology approach before MCMC was cheap.

3. **Hierarchical Bayes with latent $d_i$.** What we used. Joint
   posterior, propagates everything, exposes per-galaxy peculiar
   velocity residuals as a free byproduct, makes the modeling
   structure visible in the code itself.

4. **Hierarchical Bayes with $d_i$ analytically marginalized.**
   *Computationally* equivalent to (3); gives the same posterior on
   the population parameters. Painful in this problem because $z$ is
   linear in $d$ but $\mu$ is linear in $\log d$, so there is no
   closed-form integral; one would need 1-D numerical quadrature per
   galaxy in Stan.

The (3) vs (4) distinction is implementation convenience, not
modeling. The genuine alternatives to the Bayesian approach are (1),
which is what most introductory astronomy courses teach, and (2),
which is correct but classical. The hierarchical Bayesian path
generalizes most cleanly when more structure is added (selection
effects, candle calibration uncertainty, peculiar-velocity
correlations) — so it's where modern cosmology lives.

## Appendix C — Reproducing this

Repository layout:

```
src/
  generate.py          synthetic catalog
  fit.py               Stan fitting wrapper
  figures.py           per-fit figures
  compare.py           cross-fit figures
  models/
    complete.stan      multiplicative + q0 + fitted σ_obs
    additive.stan      drop multiplicative
    no_q0.stan         counter-example: drop q0 correction
    fixed_obs.stan     additive + σ_obs as data
    no_mu.stan         drop μ likelihood (degeneracy demo)
build/                 ephemeral output (Stan chains, summaries)
doc/                   this document and committed figures
```

Pipeline (recipes in `justfile`, run from repo root):

```bash
just generate           # write build/catalog.json
python src/fit.py --tag complete    # or additive, no_q0, ...
python src/figures.py --tag complete
python src/compare.py
just pdf                # render this document
```

Default settings are listed in Section 2. All fits in this document
use seed 1 and $N = 500$. The full toolchain (Python 3.14, CmdStan,
Pandoc, TeXLive) is pinned via `flake.nix`; entering the development
environment via `direnv allow` (or `nix develop`) gives a
deterministic shell.
